Adaptive-optics simulation results

From wavefront to image

I followed the light from Shack–Hartmann spots on the detector to the corrected image of a star. These recordings show where the correction worked and where the loop lost lock.

Explore the 2 m loop →

What the simulations show

  • In the 2.2 m loop, reconstruction stopped at 0.981 s as spots dropped out. I increased the TSVD cutoff from 0.001 to 0.05 and replayed the same atmosphere. All 1,500 frames reconstructed over two seconds and the mirror stayed within its stroke limits.
  • In the 42 m replay, four faint spots on the image diagonals came from checkerboard mirror commands that the Shack–Hartmann sensor barely detects. Filtering 96 such commands out of every update brought the corner light down to the halo level and raised the peak Strehl from 0.754 to 0.767. Three held-out atmospheres gave the same result.
  • At 1.65 µm, moving the science target from the guide star to 20 arcsec away lowered the long-exposure Strehl from 0.448 to 0.338. I used the same mirror commands for both targets.
  • With two frames of delay, raising the loop gain from 0.2 to 1.0 increased the ratio of corrected to uncorrected wavefront RMS from 0.723 to 1.746. At the higher gain the correction amplified the disturbance.
  • Increasing the photon count from 1,000 to 100,000 per subaperture reduced the RMS error of the accepted spot positions from 0.199 to 0.0194 pixels.
  • In the 8 m MCAO model, 63.5% of the on-axis H-band light fell within 50 milliarcseconds. Tomography accounted for about half of the residual variance.

Methods & evidence

What is in the model?

I calculate where the lenslet spots land, use their displacements to estimate a mirror correction, and propagate the remaining wavefront to the science image.

Atmosphere and optics

Frozen-flow phase screens translate across the pupil. The 2 m demo uses one screen; the 42 m model combines ten von Kármán layers. The sensor propagates lenslet spots to a detector model. DM shapes and reconstructors differ between configurations.

Atmosphere and implementation details →

Timing and image metrics

Each frame measures the residual with the mirror command already in place. For a long exposure I average intensities at fixed detector coordinates, then measure the peak. Image motion therefore affects long-exposure Strehl.

Limitations

Closed-loop tests

The 2.2 m runs share one two-second atmosphere, including a slow dome layer. The traces show how weakly sensed modes amplified the commands as spots dropped out. The noise fluctuation that started the sequence remains unidentified. Only the stronger cutoff acted in the revised run; the recovery rule stayed inactive.

For the 42 m gain and delay comparisons I used three paired atmospheric realizations. Frames within each run are correlated. The late rise at three frames of delay occurred in r2. Its cause is unresolved, and the saved early and late mean PSFs are available at gain 0.2 only. The damping calculation isolates static detector noise. In the faint-star run, changing flux also changed the measurement noise and the calibration.

I chose the cutoff of the 42 m waffle filter on one atmosphere and checked it on three more. All four share the turbulence profile, guide-star flux, geometry and run length. The overview image and the 42 m replay still show the earlier reconstructor.

Telescopes and images

These are simulated telescopes. The 2.2 m pupil, mirror and camera are approximations and the model has no separate tip–tilt mirror. The 42 m geometry is the historical E-ELT design. Wind, mirror stroke, control settings and wavelength also change between the 2 m and 42 m runs. Their effect on the difference in Strehl remains coupled to aperture. The ±100 µm OPD stroke belongs to the 42 m model. Applying a published mechanical stroke to a mirror requires its travel convention and angle of incidence. Hardware calibration and segment phasing require separate measurements.

For the Hubble and Webb image examples I convolved an observed template with the simulated PSF. The template carries its own resolution and noise. The Westerlund 2 example applies one on-axis PSF across its 4.109″ field. Light from bright stars outside the crop contributes near its edges.

Numerical checks

The 2 m sensor exceeded the small-signal linearity tolerance. In the historical 42 m run the iterative solver missed its command-space criterion. The gain study uses the Fourier-reference half-light radius because the production EE50 depends on focal sampling. Numerical checks and subsequent tests

What I checked

  • 2 m sensing. I applied a known tilt and compared the spot positions with the expected displacement. The response changed slightly with tilt amplitude.
  • 2 m fitting. On a static tilt, the fit to sensor measurements left 68% of the original RMS. Fitting the same 13 actuators directly to the pupil wavefront left 37%.
  • 42 m image width. I compared the production EE50 with a Fourier-interpolated measurement of the same image. The difference led me to use the Fourier value for the gain comparison.

    Why does the 42 m atmosphere look steadier?
    

    The 42 m wavefront kept moving even when its RMS barely changed. I selected a fixed 2 m patch inside the large pupil and measured how its RMS varied over the same times as the 2 m baseline.
    

    

    Comparing the whole-pupil RMS
    

    I compared each whole-pupil RMS with its value 40 ms later, dividing the larger by the smaller. The largest ratio was 1.51 for the 2 m pupil and 1.009 for the 42 m pupil. At 100 ms the corresponding ratios were 2.15 and 1.021.
    

    Both recordings last one second.
    I used all 1,000 frames of the 2 m recording and all 500 frames of the 42 m recording to compare equal time intervals.
    A layer moving at 10 m/s travels five 2 m pupil diameters in one second, or 0.24 of a 42 m diameter. A whole-pupil RMS therefore averages over different spatial scales.
    The original displays use different scales.
    The 2 m replay uses a ±1.95 µm OPD scale. The 42 m replay uses ±6.7 µm and averages each 4 × 4 group of pixels for display.
    Subtracting the 42 m wavefront at 0.200 s from the one at 0.700 s gave a difference RMS of 1,851 nm, even though the individual frames had similar RMS values.
    

    

    

    Comparing the same 2 m footprint
    

    I placed a 2 m circle at (10, 0) m inside the clear part of the 42 m pupil. At each matched time I sampled this patch on the same 52 × 52 grid as the 2 m baseline, removed piston and kept tilt. Both maps use the same ±2,500 nm OPD colour scale.
    

    I used bilinear interpolation to sample the 42 m wavefront on the 2 m grid.

    How much does the DM stroke limit matter?
    

    I compared the requested mirror commands with the configured limits at each update. Over one second the 2 m loop clipped commands in 306 of 1,000 updates. All 500 updates of the 42 m loop stayed inside the limit.
    

    Why are the limits 100× apart?
    I kept the ±1 µm OPD limit of the 2 m profile. For the 42 m run I used a much wider bound, ±100 µm OPD, to let the loop move freely.
    Clipping checks
    An update counts as clipped when at least one requested actuator command exceeds its limit. I compared the commands the mirror received with the wavefront and Strehl in the next exposure.

    The paper quotes 100 µm of actuator stroke. To compare that number with an OPD command limit, consider a mirror with 100 µm total mechanical travel, centred on its operating point and illuminated at normal incidence.
    

    100 µm mechanical total travel → ±50 µm mirror displacement → ±100 µm OPD.
    

    For a surface displacement h and angle of incidence θ, reflection changes the optical path by 2h cos θ. The conversion above illustrates those conventions. The published stroke alone does not specify M4’s usable symmetric OPD range.

    Why the 2.2 m loop lost lock
    Compare the imagesTrace the failureInspect telemetryCompare the two runsOff-axis Strehl
    

    In my 2.2 m natural-guide-star simulation, the first run stopped reconstructing the wavefront at 0.981 s. I raised the relative cutoff in the truncated singular-value decomposition (TSVD) from 0.001 to 0.05 and ran the same two seconds of atmosphere again.

    Tracing the loss of lock
    

      

    1. Spot measurements drop out. Some spots first fell below the peak signal-to-noise threshold. As the residual grew, more centroids became uncertain or their spots extended beyond the measurement window.
      2. 

    2. The remaining rows constrain the mirror less well. After dropping the rejected rows, the reconstructor still kept modes with small singular values. Inverting those values turned small spot errors into large changes in the requested mirror shape.
      3. 

    3. I checked the large increment at frame 732. Its norm over all actuator channels was 45.82 µm OPD. Applying the 0.05 cutoff to that frame reduced the norm to 5.74 µm. The weak modes accounted for 98.4% of its squared norm.
      4. 

    4. The loop stops updating the mirror. At frame 736, only 278 of 374 measurement rows remained. This fell below the 75% required for reconstruction. The last queued commands passed through the controller at frame 737. From exposure 738, eight actuators stayed at their stroke limits.
      5.

      Model families on this site
      

        

      • Native time-domain SCAO (the 2 m experiment, R1–R5 and the large-aperture convergence studies of R8): a frame-by-frame closed loop with a Shack-Hartmann sensor, a deformable mirror, a TSVD or sparse reconstructor and a leaky-integrator controller with queued delay. Detector-level profiles add a detector model (photon, read and sky noise, calibration products).
        • 

      • HCIPy time-domain components (R7): an optical backend compared with the native one on a small test configuration; both loops use the same dense reconstructor.
        • 

      • Analytical MCAO adapter (R6 and the analytical-study checks of R8): P3 sampled correction-band PSD plus a continuous fitting-tail completion propagated by MASTSEL. It supplies analytical sensitivity results alongside the native time-domain studies.
        • 

      

      Wavelengths, residual conventions, noise settings, profiles, sampling windows and budgets differ between these families and between studies; each study retains its own definitions.
      

      The 2 m experiment
      

        

        Observable definitions
        

          

        • Residual RMS: pupil RMS of the pre-update residual (what the WFS measured) after removing the pupil mean, in nm OPD. Mean per-frame RMS and the root of the mean square are reported separately.
          • 

        • Residual RMS after tip/tilt removal: for each frame, a plane a + b x + c y is fitted by equal-weight least squares over the illuminated pupil samples to the same pre-update residual map, in the pupil's own coordinates; the diagnostic is the RMS of the residual minus that plane, in nm OPD (float64, pupil exterior excluded). It is computed from the saved maps after the run and changes no image. The atmosphere RMS and the total residual RMS keep their tilt.
          • 

        • Peak Strehl: peak angular surface brightness of an image over that of the zero-OPD image of the same propagator and grid. The mean of instantaneous Strehl and the Strehl of the averaged image are different quantities and are labelled as such.
          • 

        • Averaged image: the equal-weight mean of every instantaneous intensity in the analysis window: frames 200–999 (800 frames) in the 2 m replay, and frames 100–499 (400 frames) in the 42 m replay. The window start was declared before analysis. Intensities are averaged with tilt retained and fixed image coordinates.
          • 

        • Validity: measured fractions of valid measurement rows and sub-apertures, and whether the reconstructor returned an estimate. The reconstruction gate and row fractions are reported separately.
          •

              Tracing the spots
              

                

              1. The spots sit where a checkerboard sends its light. A mirror pattern that alternates sign from each actuator to the next along both axes diffracts light to (±λ/2d, ±λ/2d). For d = 0.5 m at 2.2 µm, that is 0.454″ on each axis, exactly where the spots appeared. Makidon et al. describe the same lattice of PSF copies in AEOS images.
                2. 

              2. The sensor hardly sees that checkerboard. In the Fried geometry each lenslet measures the slopes set by the four actuators at its corners. The combination x₀₀ − x₁₀ − x₀₁ + x₁₁ produces no slope at all. A checkerboard across the mirror registers only where the pupil edges cut the lenslets. Its least sensed form, tapered toward the edges, responds at 2.2% of the typical actuator response. Tip and tilt respond at 2.7%.
                3. 

              3. The damping was too weak to hold it back. With α = 0.001, damping changes the reconstructed amplitude of that mode by 0.2%. The solve amplifies it about 45 times more than a mode of median response, and the integrator, with no leak, keeps what it receives. Over 0.2–1.0 s the mirror commands carried 49.9 nm RMS of checkerboard per actuator.
                4. 

              4. It is a family, not one mode. Multiplying any smooth command by the checkerboard gives a twin that a complete lenslet sees with the same slope energy. Every smooth correction the loop applies therefore has a checkerboard twin of nearly equal response. Twins with slowly varying envelopes send their light to points within a few λ/D of the corners.
                5. 

              

              I did not isolate what drives the family. Measurement noise and aliased high-order turbulence, fitted with large amplitudes in nearly blind directions, are the likely sources.

              Limits, sources and downloads
              

              

              Each evaluation covers 0.8 s of a 1 s run. I chose the cutoff on frames 50–149 of seed 1, which overlap that seed's evaluation window. The held-out seeds avoid that overlap but share its turbulence profile, guide-star flux, geometry and run length. Other turbulence, fluxes and longer runs are untested.
              

              The family is computed once per run from the fully valid calibration. A direction that becomes weakly sensed only when a frame drops slope rows is not in it. The cutoff of 0.35, the band factor of 1.5 and the threshold of 0.5 are my choices for this geometry, not published values. The filter exists only for the sparse LSMR reconstructor. The overview image and the 42 m replay still show version 1.
              

              Makidon et al. (2005) show that a Fried-geometry Shack–Hartmann sensor does not sense waffle, and that waffle error copies the PSF onto a square lattice at odd multiples of λ/2d. Gavel (2003) shows that Tikhonov regularization leaves the mode space unchanged, that a penalty on the global waffle mode alone leaves local waffle free, and that almost every singular mode carries some local waffle. The twin split is my own way of separating those modes; neither paper gives a cutoff for this system.
